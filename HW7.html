PART 1 · CONVERGENCE

Four meanings of "Xn → X"

A random variable is a function X(ω) of the outcome ω, so "getting close" can be measured per outcome, in probability, on average, or only through the distribution.

PART 1 · CONVERGENCE

How the modes relate

  • a.s. ⇒ P: if the paths eventually stay within ε, the probability of being outside at time n vanishes (dominated convergence).
  • L² ⇒ P: Chebyshev, P(|Xn − X| > ε) ≤ E[(Xn − X)²]/ε².
  • P ⇒ d: if Xn is close to X with high probability, their CDFs are close.
  • d ⇒ P holds only when the limit is a constant c (used in Slutsky's theorem).
  • No other implication holds in general. The next three slides show counterexamples by simulation: P ⇏ a.s., P ⇏ L², d ⇏ P. a.s. and L² do not imply each other.
COUNTEREXAMPLE 1

In probability, but not almost surely independent Xn ~ Bernoulli(pn)

Each dot is a time n with Xn = 1 on that path.

pn = 1/n: P(|Xn| > ε) = 1/n → 0, so Xn → 0 in probability. But Σ 1/n = ∞, and by the second Borel–Cantelli lemma (independence) Xn = 1 infinitely often on almost every path. The paths never settle, so there is no a.s. convergence. Ones keep appearing at every scale (about ln N per path).
pn = 1/n²: Σ 1/n² < ∞, so by the first Borel–Cantelli lemma only finitely many ones occur. After a random time every path is 0 forever: a.s. convergence.

COUNTEREXAMPLE 2

In probability, but not in mean square Xn = n with probability 1/n, else 0

P(|Xn| > ε) = 1/n → 0    but    E[Xn²] = n² · (1/n) = n → ∞

The deviation becomes rare but huge. Convergence in probability ignores how big the rare errors are. Mean-square convergence does not. With Yn = √n·𝟙 (same probability) E[Yn²] = 1 does not tend to 0 either, while Zn = 1 with probability 1/n has E[Zn²] = 1/n → 0. Bounded or uniformly integrable sequences are the ones for which "in probability" upgrades to Lp.

COUNTEREXAMPLE 3

In distribution, but not in probability X ~ N(0,1), Xn = −X for every n

By symmetry −X ~ N(0,1), so FXn = FX for every n: Xn → X in distribution trivially. Yet |Xn − X| = 2|X| for every n, so P(|Xn − X| > ε) = P(|X| > ε/2) does not depend on n and does not vanish: no convergence in probability.

Convergence in distribution compares histograms, never outcomes. That is exactly what the CLT needs: it says the law of the standardised mean becomes normal. It does not say that the standardised mean of a given data stream settles down to some value.

ROLE IN THE LLN

Law of Large Numbers: X̄n → μ

Weak LLN (convergence in probability). If Var X = σ² < ∞, then Var X̄n = σ²/n, and Chebyshev gives

P(|X̄n − μ| > ε) ≤ σ² / (n ε²) → 0.

The same computation is L² convergence: E(X̄n − μ)² = σ²/n → 0.

Strong LLN (Kolmogorov, almost sure). For i.i.d. X with E|X| < ∞, each path X̄n(ω) converges to μ. Top chart: every path enters the band and stays. Bottom chart: the share of paths outside the band at time n, the weak-LLN probability.

With Cauchy data, E|X| = ∞ and X̄n is again Cauchy for every n. There is no LLN: big jumps keep resetting the paths.

ROLE IN THE CLT

Central Limit Theorem: √n (X̄n − μ)/σ ⇒ N(0,1)

Lindeberg–Lévy: for i.i.d. X with mean μ and finite variance σ², the standardised mean Zn = √n(X̄n − μ)/σ converges in distribution to N(0,1), whatever the shape of X.

The speed depends on skewness. Berry–Esseen gives sup|Fn − Φ| ≤ 0.4748·E|X−μ|³/(σ³√n), so skewed or discrete data need larger n. The lower chart shows the measured Kolmogorov distance against n.

Convergence is only in distribution: on one data stream Zn and Z2n have correlation 1/√2 for every n, so Zn(ω) keeps fluctuating and never converges. What stabilises is the histogram over many streams. That is why the CLT justifies confidence intervals and tests (statements about the procedure), not predictions about one realisation.
THE ROLE OF √n

Why √n? Because independent variances add

Var(Sn) = Σi Var Xi + 2 Σi<j Cov(Xi, Xj)

If the Xi are independent (uncorrelated is enough), the covariances vanish, Var Sn = nσ², and the standard deviation grows like σ√n. Consequences:

  • Sn/n has sd σ/√n → 0: the LLN scale (1/n is "too strong", it kills the randomness).
  • Sn/√n has sd σ for every n: the only scaling with a non-degenerate limit, the CLT scale.
  • The standard error σ/√n: to halve the error you need four times the data.

When independence fails, √n is no longer the right rate. Positive serial correlation (AR(1)) keeps the √n rate but inflates the constant by √((1+ρ)/(1−ρ)), so a naive standard error is too small. A common shock shared by all observations (equicorrelation c) gives Var Sn = nσ² + n(n−1)cσ² ~ n², and averaging never removes it. With infinite variance (Cauchy) the scale is n1/α, not √n.

PART 2 · BAYES

Conditional probability and Bayes' theorem

P(A | B) = P(A ∩ B) / P(B),   P(B) > 0

Conditioning restricts the sample space to the outcomes where B happened and renormalises. Writing P(A ∩ B) in two ways gives Bayes' theorem. With a partition {Hk} (law of total probability):

P(H | D) = P(D | H) · P(H) / P(D),    P(D) = Σk P(D | Hk) P(Hk)

termmeaning
prior P(H)belief about the hypothesis before seeing the data
likelihood P(D | H)how well H predicts the observed data (a function of H)
evidence P(D)normalising constant: the probability of D averaged over all hypotheses
posterior P(H | D)updated belief after the data
Odds form: posterior odds = likelihood ratio × prior odds. Every new independent piece of evidence multiplies the odds by its own likelihood ratio, so the posterior of today is the prior of tomorrow.
For a parameter θ with data x: π(θ | x) ∝ L(θ; x) · π(θ). Inference is then a probability distribution over θ. Its mean or mode gives a point estimate, its quantiles give a credible interval, and integrals give the probability of any hypothesis such as P(θ > 0.1 | x).

Contrast with frequentist inference: there θ is fixed and probabilities refer to repeated samples (p-values, confidence intervals, the CLT of Part 1). In Bayesian inference the data are fixed and the uncertainty is about θ.

EXAMPLE 2 · LEARNING A PARAMETER

What fraction θ of staff click a phishing link? Beta–Binomial model

θ ~ Beta(a, b),   k | θ ~ Bin(n, θ)  ⇒  θ | k ~ Beta(a + k, b + n − k)

The prior acts like a + b pseudo-observations, a of them clicks. The posterior mean (a + k)/(a + b + n) is a weighted average of the prior mean and the sample proportion k/n. The data's weight n/(a+b+n) → 1 as n grows.

"Stream data" adds simulated emails one batch at a time. The posterior narrows like 1/√n around the true θ: the Bernstein–von Mises theorem, Part 1's √n reappearing in Bayesian inference.

PART 2 · DISCUSSION

Bayesian inference: strengths, limits and the influence of the prior

Advantages

  • Coherent updating: one rule (Bayes) for combining evidence. It is naturally sequential, which suits streams of alerts and logs.
  • Uses prior knowledge: base rates, expert judgement and past campaigns. This is decisive when data are scarce (rare attacks, new threats).
  • Direct answers: "P(attack | alert) = 9%" and "θ ∈ [0.14, 0.31] with probability 95%" are statements about what we care about, unlike p-values.
  • Decision-ready: posteriors combine with costs (expected loss) to set alert thresholds.

Limitations

  • Prior choice is a modelling decision that can be contested. With little data it drives the conclusion (try "strong prior" on the previous slide with n = 5).
  • Sensitivity analysis is required: report how conclusions change under reasonable priors.
  • Computation: beyond conjugate models, posteriors need MCMC or variational methods.
  • Model misspecification is not fixed by Bayes: a wrong likelihood gives a confident wrong posterior.
  • Base-rate neglect by humans (previous slide): even with the right formula, results are counter-intuitive and must be explained.
As n → ∞ the likelihood dominates any prior that does not exclude the truth, and the posterior concentrates at rate 1/√n around the true value (posterior consistency). The two parts meet here: Bayesian learning is itself a convergence theorem.
SUMMARY

Take-aways

  • a.s. and L² each imply convergence in probability, which implies convergence in distribution. The converses fail (Borel–Cantelli, rare huge errors, Xn = −X).
  • LLN: X̄n → μ in probability (weak) and almost surely (strong), at rate σ/√n.
  • CLT: √n(X̄n − μ)/σ → N(0,1) in distribution only.
  • √n comes from adding independent variances. Correlation changes the constant or even the rate.
  • Bayes: posterior ∝ likelihood × prior; the posterior odds are the prior odds times the likelihood ratio.
  • Base rates matter: a 99%-sensitive detector can still produce mostly false alarms.
  • Conjugate updating (Beta–Binomial) shows the prior as pseudo-data whose weight fades as n grows.
  • Priors are a strength when they encode real knowledge and a risk when they don't, so always check sensitivity.
