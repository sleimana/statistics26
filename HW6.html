5 · Independence in probability and in statistics

In probability. Events A and B are independent when P(A ∩ B) = P(A)·P(B), equivalently P(A | B) = P(A): learning B does not change the probability of A. Two discrete random variables X and F are independent when the joint distribution factorises for every pair of values, P(X = xi, F = fj) = P(X = xi)·P(F = fj). Then every conditional distribution of F given X equals the marginal of F. This is a property of the population model, and it is either exactly true or false.

In statistics. We only see a sample, so we use the empirical analogue: X and F are empirically independent when fij = fi··f·j, i.e. all row profiles are equal. In real data this almost never holds exactly, because random sampling produces small deviations even when the population variables are independent. The question becomes whether the deviations are larger than chance would produce. Pearson's χ² measures the total deviation between observed counts and the counts expected under independence. Under H₀ (independence), and when the expected counts are not too small (rule of thumb eij ≥ 5), χ² approximately follows a chi-square distribution with (r−1)(c−1) degrees of freedom. A large χ² (small p-value) is evidence against independence. A small χ² does not prove independence: it only says the data are compatible with it. Effect size is a separate question. Cramér's V = √(χ²/(n·(min(r,c)−1))) rescales χ² to [0, 1], because with thousands of observations even tiny, irrelevant differences become "significant".
