The two definitions, numerically

Riemann:   ∫ab f ≈ Σi=1N f(ξi)·Δx,   ξi ∈ [xi−1, xi]
Lebesgue (layer-cake form):   ∫ f dμ = ∫0∞ μ{x : f(x) > y} dy ≈ Σj=1K Δy · μ{f > yj},   yj = jΔy

The Lebesgue sum groups points by value: Σj yj·μ(Ej) with Ej = {yj ≤ f < yj+1}. Summing by parts gives the layer-cake sum above, the lower Lebesgue sum. It always needs the measure of a preimage. For monotone or piecewise-monotone f the preimage is an interval, and we compute it exactly (for sin x / x by bisection). In general it has to be estimated, and the "fine grid" option does that by counting grid points. That hidden cost is a Riemann sum in disguise.

What the experiments show

Accuracy and convergence. For continuous, piecewise-smooth functions both methods converge to the same value, as the theory says: Riemann-integrable ⇒ Lebesgue-integrable with the same integral. The midpoint Riemann rule has error O(N⁻²) for smooth f (slope −2 in the log–log chart), and the left/right rules have O(N⁻¹). The lower Lebesgue sum with exact preimages has error O(K⁻¹) (it is a left rule in y), and it is insensitive to how wild f is in x. A jump costs Riemann O(N⁻¹) unless a grid point falls on it, while for Lebesgue a jump is just a flat stretch of μ{f > y}. For 1/√x Riemann sees an unbounded integrand: left endpoints fail, and midpoints converge only like N−1/2. Lebesgue integrates μ{f > y} = min(1, y−2), which is bounded. With geometrically spaced levels up to y = K, the truncation (1/K) and discretisation (≈ 2 ln K / K) errors are both small, and the unboundedness costs nothing special.

Efficiency. Riemann needs N evaluations of f and nothing else. Lebesgue needs K preimage measurements. They are cheap when the inverse is known in closed form, but the estimated version needs M ≫ K evaluations of f to be accurate, so in practice it is slower (see "evals" in the table). This is why numerical libraries are Riemann-like (quadrature rules), while Lebesgue's idea matters for theory: limits, probability and expectations of random variables.

Functions where they behave differently

Dirichlet (1 on ℚ, 0 elsewhere). Every subinterval contains rationals and irrationals, so the upper Riemann sums are all 1 and the lower ones 0. The Riemann sum depends on the tags: 1 for rational midpoints, 0 for "irrational" tags. The function is not Riemann integrable. Lebesgue: ℚ ∩ [0,1] is countable and has measure 0, so μ{f > y} = 0 for y ≥ 0 and the integral is 0. (A computer cannot sample irrationals, because every float is rational. The tags are therefore handled symbolically.)

Thomae (1/q at p/q in lowest terms, 0 on irrationals). It is discontinuous on every rational but continuous on the irrationals, so the discontinuities have measure zero and the function is Riemann integrable, with integral 0. Rational midpoint sums converge to 0 only slowly, as the chart shows. Lebesgue gets 0 at once, since {f > y} is finite for every y > 0.

Why Φ has no elementary formula (Liouville)

Φ(x) = ∫−∞x φ(t) dt,   φ(t) = e−t²/2/√(2π)

Liouville's theorem (1835, later made algorithmic by Risch): for rational functions f, g with g non-constant, an antiderivative of f·eg is elementary if and only if there is a rational function R with R′ + g′R = f, and then ∫f eg = R eg. For φ take f = 1 and g = −t²/2, so we need R′ − tR = 1. If R were a polynomial of degree d, then tR would have degree d+1 and nothing could cancel its leading term. If R had a pole of order m at some c, then R′ would have a pole of order m+1 that tR cannot cancel. So no rational R exists, and e−t²/2 has no elementary antiderivative. No finite combination of polynomials, exp, log, roots and trigonometric functions equals Φ.

The error function: exact, but still numerical

erf(z) = (2/√π) ∫0z e−t² dt,    Φ(x) = ½ [1 + erf(x/√2)] = ½ erfc(−x/√2)

Defining a new special function gives an exact closed form for Φ, much as "log" names the antiderivative of 1/x. But erf is itself defined by the integral. Evaluating it still means summing a series, a continued fraction or a fitted rational approximation, so numerical methods remain necessary. The special function packages the computation, it does not remove it.

The methods implemented here

Alternating Taylor series of erf: exact in principle, but for |x| ≳ 5 the terms grow to about ex²/2 before shrinking, and cancellation destroys the digits. Positive (Kummer) series erf(z) = (2/√π)e−z²Σ 2kz2k+1/(2k+1)!!: no cancellation, but it needs about z² + 30 terms. Simpson's rule on φ from 0 to x: error O(N⁻⁴), and it costs N evaluations of exp. Laplace continued fraction for the upper tail 1 − Φ(x) = φ(x)/(x + 1/(x + 2/(x + 3/(x + …)))): converges fast for large x and computes tiny tails directly, avoiding the catastrophic 1 − Φ cancellation. Hybrid (my method): Kummer series for |x| < 3, continued fraction for |x| ≥ 3, symmetry Φ(−x) = 1 − Φ(x) applied to the small side. It reaches full double precision (relative error ≈ 10⁻¹⁵ even at x = −8) in about 30–60 operations. Abramowitz–Stegun 7.1.26: a 5-term rational-exponential fit with absolute error below 1.5·10⁻⁷. It is very fast and good enough for most statistics, but useless for extreme tails.
