Systems and attackers: the aggregate picture

How the system size n and the number of attackers m shape what a defender observes.

Model and interpretation

The model

Attacker j ∈ {1,…,m} probes systems i = 1,…,n in sequence. On each system it gains a score increment Xi(j), i.i.d. across systems and attackers. Its running score after k systems is a random walk:

Sk(j) = X1(j) + … + Xk(j),   μ = E[X],   σ² = Var X

Bernoulli: μ = p, σ² = p(1−p). A step is a successful penetration (1) or a failure (0), so Sn ~ Binomial(n, p) counts breached systems. Rademacher: μ = 0, σ² = 1. Each step moves the attacker forward or backward (a gained or lost foothold), the symmetric zero-drift case.

The three quantities

E[Sn] = nμ, Var Sn = nσ²  |  E[Sn/n] = μ, Var = σ²/n  |  E[Sn/√n] = √n μ, Var = σ²

Variances of independent terms add, so the total score spreads like √n. Dividing by n shrinks the spread like 1/√n, and that is the Law of Large Numbers: Sn/n → μ in probability (Chebyshev gives P(|Sn/n − μ| > ε) ≤ σ²/(nε²)). Dividing by √n is the unique scaling that keeps the variance constant. The Central Limit Theorem says the shape then converges: in the Rademacher case Sn/√n → N(0,1) in distribution. In the Bernoulli case Sn/√n has mean p√n → ∞, so it must be centred: (Sn − np)/√(np(1−p)) → N(0,1).

What it means for systems and attackers

• More systems (n ↑): the total damage of each attacker becomes larger and more spread out in absolute terms (±σ√n). The rate Sn/n becomes predictable: on a large infrastructure every attacker converges to the same breach rate p, so p, not luck, decides the outcome. In the symmetric case the average progress is zero, but individual attackers still wander ±√n away. Big infrastructures show bigger extremes even with no drift.

• More attackers (m ↑) does not change the law of any single trajectory. It gives more independent samples, so the histograms, the LLN probabilities and the KS distances are estimated more precisely (noise ∝ 1/√m). It also changes the aggregate: the campaign total ΣjSn(j) has mean m n μ and standard deviation σ√(mn). The worst-case attacker, the maximum over m, grows roughly like nμ + σ√n·√(2 ln m). A defender facing many attackers must plan for this tail, not for the average.
